The young one watched the elder's fingers roll the cigarette. The fire crackled between them. Finally, the young one spoke.

"You ever been down in that canyon?"

The elder didn't answer right away. He lit the cigarette, watched the smoke curl up toward stars that had seen this conversation a thousand times before.

"Every man's been in a canyon," he said. "Question is whether you walked down or got pushed."

"What's the difference?"

"The difference is whether you're still looking for someone to blame when you get to the bottom."

The young one considered this. The horses shifted in the dark. Somewhere far off, a coyote tested the silence.

"There's a bruja in that canyon," the young one said. "Folks talk about her."

"Folks talk about a lot of things."

"You know her?"

The elder drew on the cigarette. The ember glowed.

"I've met a lot of brujas," he said. "Some of 'em wore wedding dresses. Some of 'em wore nothing at all. Some of 'em looked exactly like my own reflection in still water."

The young one waited.

"The bruja in YOUR canyon," the elder continued, "ain't the same as the bruja in mine. That's the thing nobody tells you. You go down there looking for someone else's witch, you'll never find your way back up."

"How do you know when you're ready to go down?"

The elder crushed the cigarette under his boot.

"You don't know. You just go. Or you don't. But either way, the canyon don't forget you been standing at the edge. The land remembers everything."

He rolled into his blanket, his back to the fire.

"Get some sleep. We ride East at dawn."

The young one sat with the fire for a long time after that, watching the embers die down to coals, thinking about canyons and brujas and all the things the elder hadn't said.

And somewhere in the dark, the bruja waited.

She always waited.
